## Where It Starts
OpenCut for Windows sits at the point where raw footage becomes a finished video — the timeline where clips get trimmed, reordered, and layered with text before export. People come to it from very different starting points: a creator assembling a highlight reel, someone editing a short clip for social media, or a hobbyist experimenting with editing for the first time. What using OpenCut looks like day to day is mostly this: import footage, arrange it, trim the parts that don't belong, and export.

> Open development tends to shape a tool in small, visible increments rather than big unannounced leaps — anyone curious can watch the codebase change over time instead of waiting for a version number to tell the story.

## What You'll Find Once You're In
Once a project is open, most of the day-to-day work in OpenCut happens across a handful of tools that tend to matter most:

- A multi-track timeline for arranging video, audio, and image clips together
- Trim and split controls for cutting footage down to the parts worth keeping
- Basic text overlays for captions or simple titles
- Export options for turning the finished timeline into a shareable video file

## What It Asks of Your Machine
OpenCut doesn't demand exotic hardware — a Windows 10 or newer 64-bit machine with a modern processor and a few gigabytes of memory is generally enough to work comfortably, and free storage should account for both the app itself and whatever video footage a project pulls in.

## Settling It In
Getting OpenCut installed is a short detour rather than a project of its own: the installer downloads, runs through a handful of familiar setup screens, and hands off to the app itself once it's done. The first launch usually takes a moment or two, mostly spent getting the workspace ready rather than asking for anything unusual from the person installing it.

## The First Few Minutes
1. OpenCut opens to an empty project, waiting for footage to be dropped in rather than presenting a wall of options up front.
2. Importing clips and dragging them onto the timeline is usually the first real action, and that's where the shape of the eventual video starts to appear.
3. From there, trimming, reordering, and exporting settle into a rhythm — the point where onboarding quietly ends and ordinary editing begins.

> First-run moments tend to matter more than they get credit for: a workspace that makes sense in the first five minutes usually stays comfortable for the following hundred hours of use.
